The AfD won a German state election on 6 September, and the question that follows is not whether it came first. It is whether its candidate can be sworn in with 39 seats out of 83, and that turns on a voting procedure that most coverage summarises incorrectly.

## What happened on 6 September?

The AfD took **43.8%** and **39 of the 83 seats** in the Saxony-Anhalt Landtag, the strongest result for a party classified as far-right in a German state election since 1945. A majority needs **42**. It fell three short.

| Party | Seats |
| --- | --- |
| **AfD** | **39** |
| CDU | 15 |
| SPD | 8 |
| Greens | 8 |
| Left | 8 |
| **BSW** | **5** |

The summer contract on an outright AfD majority, which traded as high as 48%, went to **zero** on election night. Every other party rules out governing with the AfD — the arrangement Germans call the *Brandmauer*, the firewall.

Add the seats up and the shape of the problem appears: the AfD has 39, and the four firewall parties have **exactly 39** between them. BSW's five seats sit in the middle.

## Why does the final ballot decide this?

Article 65 of the state constitution sets out a sequence, and the last step is the one that matters:

1. **First ballot:** a candidate needs a majority of all members, 42 votes.
2. **Second ballot**, within seven days: same threshold.
3. If that fails, parliament votes within fourteen days on **ending the legislative period early** — a snap election.
4. If the dissolution vote fails, a further ballot follows immediately. Here the winner needs **a majority of the votes cast**, and **abstentions are not counted**.

That fourth step is widely described as "whoever gets the most votes wins". It is not a plurality rule: it is a majority of votes actually cast.

The difference decides the question. If BSW's five members abstain and everyone else votes, the count is **39 for and 39 against** — not a majority of votes cast, so nobody is elected. BSW abstaining is necessary for an AfD Minister-President, and on its own it is not sufficient. The AfD needs at least one more abstention, absence or defection from the other 39.

Two further details keep the price honest. **Ulrich Siegmund** said before the election that he would only stand for Minister-President if the AfD won an outright majority, and it did not. And German constitutional lawyers are actively arguing about how a final-round ballot paper should even be designed — whether it offers yes, no and abstain — which tells you the procedure has not been stress-tested in a case like this.

Two readings worth taking from that table.

**The Willingmann line is not a 6% chance; it is an absence of a price.** A 0.7c bid against an 11.7c ask on $3,700 of volume means nobody is really quoting it. Treat the midpoint as noise rather than as the market's view.

**Mecklenburg-Vorpommern is the control case.** The AfD also came first there on 20 September, with 38.2%, and its chance of entering that government is priced at 3.5% because the other parties hold a majority without it. Coming first and taking office are different questions, and the market separates them cleanly.

## Why are two markets on the same event ten cents apart?

Because one has a deadline. **AfD in the next Saxony-Anhalt government** trades at 40.5c and resolves whenever the first post-election government takes office, however long that takes. **AfD in any German state government in 2026** trades at 30.5c and needs a government formally sworn in by **31 December**.

So the ten-cent gap is not a disagreement about whether the AfD gets in. It is the price of the calendar: the market's estimate that this drags past New Year. Note also that the second market is the broader event — any of sixteen states — and still trades lower, which only makes sense because of that deadline.

That gives you a clean choice rather than a dilemma. If you think the AfD takes office but the process grinds on, the Saxony-Anhalt contract is the one. If you think the vote happens this autumn, the dated contract pays more for the same view. **Holding both is one view twice**, not a hedge.

## Three ways to take a view

| View | Contract | Price | Return |

| The AfD gets in, whenever | Siegmund as Minister-President | 41.5c | about **2.4x** |
| The AfD gets in, and this autumn | AfD in a state government in 2026 | 30.5c | about **3.3x** |

On the first: the contract has no calendar deadline of its own, but it does resolve to "Other" if nobody is sworn in by **31 May 2027**, and a caretaker does not count. Your capital can be tied up for eight months.

On the second: that 24c/37c spread is the trade. Buying at the ask means paying 37c for something quoted at 30.5c, which turns a 3.3x into 2.7x before anything happens. Use limit orders — this is exactly the case described in [why orders fill at a worse price](/learn/why-did-my-order-fill-at-a-worse-price).

On the third: this is the only deep book here, and it resolves on an *announcement* of resignation or removal, whatever date it takes effect. That makes it a faster-moving market than the state ones, and a small position rather than a core one.

## What would move these prices?

- **BSW stating its position** on the final ballot. This is the single largest input, and there is no scheduled date for it.
- **A date being set** for the Minister-President vote, which starts the seven-day and fourteen-day clocks in Article 65.
- **The dissolution vote**, the step most coverage skips. If parliament votes to end the period early, a snap election replaces the whole question.

## Before you take any of this

1. **Read the spread, not the midpoint.** Three of these markets trade on under $40,000. The quoted price is not the price you get.
2. **Check the deadline on the contract you are buying.** The same opinion is worth 41.5c, 40.5c or 30.5c depending only on which clock it runs on, and [reading the rules first](/learn/read-the-resolution-rules-first) is what separates them.
3. **Size for a long hold.** The Saxony-Anhalt contracts can run to mid-2027, so [size the position](/learn/how-to-size-a-position) as money you will not need before then.
4. Coalition politics changes without warning, and none of these markets pays anything for being directionally right on the wrong timetable.
