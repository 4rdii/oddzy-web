As of 7 October 2026 on Oddzy, the 2027 French presidential election odds put Marine Le Pen in front at 43.25¢ — a little under a coin flip — with Édouard Philippe at 17.5¢ and Jean-Luc Mélenchon at 12.5¢. The first round is on 18 April 2027 and the runoff on 2 May.

*Prices are from Oddzy at 07:12 UTC on 7 October 2026. Each is the price of a share that pays $1 if that person becomes president and $0 if not. Polls are from the Ifop-Fiducial survey for Le Figaro, LCI and Sud Radio, fieldwork 25–29 September 2026, 1,393 respondents. Market analysis, not financial advice, and not a political position.*

## Why is Le Pen not a stronger favourite?

Because two things have to go right for her, and the market doubts both a little. It prices her at 91.5¢ to appear on the official candidate list at all, and at about 47% to win if she does.

The ballot discount is the court case. The Paris Court of Appeal upheld her embezzlement conviction on 7 July 2026 and cut her ineligibility to a term she had already served, which cleared her to run. It also imposed a year under electronic monitoring. She had said she would not campaign wearing a tag; her appeal to the Court of Cassation suspends that sentence until it rules, which French reporting expects to be too late to affect the campaign.

The 47% is the runoff. Le Pen leads every first-round scenario Ifop tested, but France’s two-round system means she then faces one opponent with everyone else’s voters to compete for. The market is saying the runoff is close to even.

## Why does a mayor polling at 3.5% trade at 12¢?

Not because of the polls. David Lisnard, mayor of Cannes, polled 3.5% in Ifop’s 25–29 September survey, and polls published in September put him between 2% and 5%; his price has been driven by a burst of buying since early September. Oddzy prices him at 12.35¢ to win — level with Mélenchon, who polls four times higher.

His contract is also the most traded serious candidate on the board: $137,196 in the 24 hours to this snapshot, more than Le Pen, Philippe and Mélenchon combined. Journal du Coin reported on 6 October that his price had been around 3¢ in early September, and that three accounts placed about 40% of the money bet on him between 2 and 6 October. A price driven by a handful of buyers is what those buyers think, not what the crowd thinks.

Attal is the opposite case. He polls 15–17% when Ifop tests him as the centre’s candidate, but the market gives him only 18.5¢ to be on the ballot at all — it expects Philippe, not Attal, to carry that lane.

## How does the French election market decide who won?

Each winner market pays on whoever wins the election, runoff included, based on a consensus of credible reporting. If that is ambiguous, it uses the official results from France’s Ministry of the Interior. If the result is not known by 11:59 PM ET on 31 December 2027, every named candidate resolves NO and the market settles on “Other”.

The ballot markets are narrower. They resolve on the official first-round candidate list published by the Constitutional Council. To get on it, a candidate needs 500 signatures from elected officials in at least 30 departments. What “consensus of credible reporting” means on election night is covered in [how election prediction markets decide who won](/learn/election-prediction-markets).

## What does each side pay if you are right?

Held to the result, about seven months away, the returns look like this. These are midpoint prices; the ask you actually buy at is a little higher, and on thin contracts noticeably so.

| Position | Price | Pays | Return if right |
|---|---|---|---|
| YES on Le Pen | 43.25¢ | $1 | 131% |
| NO on Le Pen | 56.75¢ | $1 | 76% |
| YES on Philippe | 17.5¢ | $1 | 471% |
| NO on Lisnard | 87.65¢ | $1 | 14% |

The bottom row is the bet against the gap between price and polls. It is not free money. It ties up 87.65¢ for seven months to earn 12.35¢, and a candidate who surprises loses you all of it.
