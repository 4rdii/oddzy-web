A basket is a curated set of positions bought together. One tap places every leg, and your stake is split across them according to weights the author chose.

If you have used a sportsbook, this will look like a parlay. It is the opposite of one, and the difference decides what you actually own.

## How is a basket different from a parlay?

A basket is not one bet. It is several ordinary positions bought at the same moment. Each leg becomes its own position, resolves on its own schedule, and pays independently of the others. Two legs winning and three losing is a completely normal outcome — you keep what the two paid.

A parlay works the other way round. It is a single bet with several conditions: all of them must land, and if one fails the whole thing is worth nothing.

You can also sell any single leg before it resolves without disturbing the rest, exactly as you would [sell a position you bought on its own](/learn/can-you-sell-before-a-market-resolves). A parlay has no such exit; it is one ticket, alive or dead.

| | Parlay | Basket |
|---|---|---|
| What you own | One ticket with several conditions | Several independent positions |
| If one leg loses | The whole ticket pays nothing | Only that leg pays nothing |
| Settlement | All at once, at the end | Each leg on its own schedule |
| Selling early | Not possible | Any single leg, at market price |

## How is your stake split across the legs?

Every leg carries a weight — its share of whatever you put in. A basket weighted 40 / 35 / 25 puts $40, $35 and $25 of a $100 stake into its three legs.

Because each leg must clear the minimum bet, the *smallest* weight sets the basket's minimum stake. A leg weighted 10% needs the basket to be at least ten times the minimum, which is why some baskets have a higher floor than others. The floor is always shown before you buy.

Some baskets are sized differently: instead of splitting the money evenly, they buy the same **number of shares** in each leg. That matters when only one leg can win — five clubs chasing [the same trophy](/topic/ucl-winner), say. Splitting the money equally there would buy fewer shares of the favourite than of the outsider, so the favourite winning would pay less than the underdog. Buying equal shares makes every winner pay the same.

The weights are someone else's sizing decision, so it is worth reading them the way you would read [your own position sizing](/learn/how-to-size-a-position) — the number you choose for the basket as a whole is still yours.

## What happens if one leg doesn't fill?

The other legs still go through, and the unfilled leg's share stays in your balance. Legs are placed as separate orders against separate order books, so they succeed or fail independently — if one leg has no seller at the price shown, that is a problem for that leg only.

The unspent money is never quietly moved into the legs that did fill — you would end up holding more of something you never sized up. You always get a receipt showing exactly which legs filled, at what price, and how much came back unspent. Individual legs can still fill a cent or two away from the price on the card, for the [same order-book reasons a single order does](/learn/why-did-my-order-fill-at-a-worse-price).

## Why use a basket instead of buying the markets yourself?

A basket is a way to express one view with several instruments, at sizes someone has already thought about. "Rates hold through the year" is not one market — it is three or four, each capturing a different part of the same idea. A basket makes that a single decision instead of four.

Nothing stops you assembling the same positions by hand, and the outcome is identical if you do. What the [basket page](/basket) gives you is the weighting, the minimum, and one confirmation screen instead of four.
