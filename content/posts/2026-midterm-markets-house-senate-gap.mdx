Four outcomes are priced on the 2026 midterm board, and it is tempting to read them as four separate scenarios. They are not. Behind all four sits one variable — how far the national swing reaches into the Senate map — and the prices are a measurement of that one number, sliced several ways.

All figures below are from Oddzy's market data as of 11 September 2026, with polling and race ratings from the first ten days of September. Prices move daily — check the market page before acting on any of them.

## Two prices, one election

| Market | Democrat | Republican | Volume |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [House of Representatives](/market/will-the-democratic-party-control-the-house-after-the-2026-midterm-elections) | **87.5c** | 13.5c | $10.9M |
| Senate | **52.5c** | 48.5c | $4.3M |

Same election, same voters, same date. One is close to settled and the other is a coin flip, 35 cents apart.

The reason is that not every seat is on the ballot. All 435 House seats are contested, so the House price is close to a direct reading of the national mood — and that mood currently favours Democrats by about **6.6 points** on the Silver Bulletin generic-ballot average. In the Senate only about a third of the chamber votes, roughly 35 seats in 2026, and **which** third matters far more than how many.

## Why does defending more seats not mean losing more seats?

Republicans hold 53 Senate seats and are defending 22 of the seats up this year, against 13 for the Democrats. On paper that is nearly twice the exposure.

It is not twice the risk, because of where those seats are. **20 of the 22** Republican-held seats up this year are in states Trump carried by double digits in 2024, and a national swing large enough to take the House does not move most of them.

> **The clause most people skip.** If no party wins an outright majority, the market resolves to the party holding half the seats *and* the Vice Presidency. The Vice President is a Republican, so a 50-50 Senate resolves as Republican control. Democrats have to get Republicans down to **49**, a net gain of four — the kind of clause covered in [read the resolution rules first](/learn/read-the-resolution-rules-first).

## Where do Democrats actually need to win the Senate?

In exactly four places, and they lead in all four. Silver Bulletin's polling averages, updated on 6 September, put Democrats ahead in four Republican-held races — precisely the net four they need:

| State | Race | Where it stands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **North Carolina** | Roy Cooper (D) v Michael Whatley (R), open seat after Thom Tillis retired | Lean Democratic |
| **Maine** | Troy Jackson (D) v Susan Collins (R) | Toss-up; a CNN poll taken 31 Aug–6 Sept had Jackson 48–45, within the margin of error |
| **Ohio** | Sherrod Brown (D) v Jon Husted (R) | Toss-up since 3 September |
| **Texas** | James Talarico (D) v Ken Paxton (R) | Toss-up; recent polls range from Paxton +1 to Talarico +4 |

Two things make that less comfortable than it looks. The next targets are harder: Republicans lead the averages in Iowa, where Joni Ernst is retiring, and in Kansas and Florida, while Alaska — Mary Peltola against Dan Sullivan under ranked-choice voting — tilts Republican. So there is almost no spare seat.

And Democrats have to defend at the same time: Jon Ossoff in **Georgia**, plus open seats in **Michigan**, **New Hampshire** and **Minnesota**. The same averages have Democrats ahead in all four, but Michigan and New Hampshire are both rated toss-ups.

Maine is also worth reading closely: Graham Platner won the Democratic primary and withdrew in July, and Jackson was chosen at a convention on 25 July. A late nominee in a race that decides the chamber is exactly the kind of fact that moves this board in one headline.

## Why is the Senate price 52.5 cents and not lower?

Because the market is not multiplying independent races — it is pricing one swing.

Suppose each of the four pickups were a separate 65% proposition. Winning all four would be:

> 0.65 × 0.65 × 0.65 × 0.65 ≈ **18%**

— and that is *before* holding Georgia, Michigan, New Hampshire and Minnesota. If the races were independent, Democratic control would be a long shot. The market prices it at **52.5%**.

The difference is correlation. Close races in the same year are driven by the same national environment: if the generic ballot holds near D+6.6, narrow leads tend to convert together; if it tightens, they tend to slip together. The Senate market is really one bet on whether the swing clears the fourth-hardest seat. A related contract says the same thing from the other side: Republicans losing a Senate seat in *some* state Trump won in 2024 trades at **95.5c** — the market is near-certain the swing reaches North Carolina, and genuinely unsure it reaches Texas.

## What does the combined board price?

The [Balance of Power market](/topic/midterms-balance) prices the **combination** of the two chambers, with about $11.5 million of volume across its four outcomes:

| Outcome | Price | Return if right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Democratic Senate, Democratic House | **49.5c** | +102% |
| Republican Senate, Democratic House | **35.5c** | +182% |
| [Republican Senate, Republican House](/market/2026-balance-of-power-r-senate-r-house-537) | **12.5c** | +700% |
| Democratic Senate, Republican House | **1.55c** | +6,352% |

A sanity check against the standalone markets: Democrats winning the House works out to 49.5 + 35.5 = 85.0, or **85.8%** once normalised, against 87.5c standalone. Democrats winning the Senate is 49.5 + 1.55 = 51.05, or **51.5%** normalised, against 52.5c. Separate order books, agreeing to within about two points — coherent enough to reason from.

## Why is the cheapest cell the most informative?

Democratic Senate with a Republican House is priced at **1.55 cents**. The market is saying that combination effectively does not happen: any swing big enough to flip four Senate seats has already carried the House.

So the four outcomes are really one ladder with three rungs:

| Size of the swing | Outcome | Price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No swing | Republicans hold both | **12.5c** |
| Medium swing | Democrats take the House only | **35.5c** |
| Large swing | Democrats take both | **49.5c** |

**97.5 cents of the board sits on those three rungs.** One variable, three prices.

## How much is the correlation worth?

If the two chambers were independent, Democrats winning both would be the product:

> 0.875 × 0.525 = **45.9%**

The market prices it at **49.5%**. Those extra **3.6 points are the correlation**, quoted directly. The same numbers give conditional probabilities:

- Democratic Senate **if** Democrats win the House: 49.5 ÷ 85.0 = **58.2%**
- Democratic House **if** Democrats win the Senate: 49.5 ÷ 51.05 = **97.0%**
- Republican Senate **if** Republicans hold the House: 12.5 ÷ 14.05 = **89.0%**

The first is the one that matters. The Senate on its own is a coin flip; in the world the House market already expects, it is closer to three in five.

## What does the seat ladder add?

Separate contracts price each exact number of Republican Senate seats:

| Republican seats | Price | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 47 or fewer | 29.5c | Democrats |
| Exactly 48 | 15.5c | Democrats |
| Exactly 49 | 13.5c | Democrats |
| Exactly 50 | 12.0c | Republicans (via the Vice President) |
| Exactly 51 | 10.5c | Republicans |
| Exactly 52 | 4.4c | Republicans |
| Exactly 53 | 4.9c | Republicans |
| 54 to 56 | 7.05c | Republicans |
| 57 or more | 0.75c | Republicans |

The Democratic-control rungs sum to 58.5 cents, or about **60%** normalised — against **52.5c** on the control market. That is a real gap, and it is worth resisting the urge to trade it. The rungs that decide control have barely traded: 49 seats took $7 in the last 24 hours and 50 seats nothing, while the Democratic Senate control market took about $11,900. The control market is the live price; the ladder is a set of stale quotes nobody has updated.

## Three ways to express a view

### Do not pay for what everyone already knows

If your view is "the swing is real", buying the House is the weakest way to say it:

| Contract | Price | Return | What it needs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Democratic House | 87.5c | **+14%** | a swing |
| Democratic Senate | 52.5c | **+90%** | a swing that clears all four seats |
| Democrats take both | 49.5c | **+102%** | the same |

Roughly seven times the payout for nearly the same opinion. The House price is consensus; the genuine disagreement is in the Senate.

### The correlation trap

Buying "Democratic House" and "Democratic Senate" separately feels like two positions. It is one view held twice. At 100 contracts of each, the cost is 140 cents:

| Scenario | Probability | Bought separately | Both-chambers only (49.5c) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Democrats take both | 49.5% | 200 → **+60** | 100 → **+50.5** |
| Split government | 35.5% | 100 → **−40** | 0 → **−49.5** |
| Republicans hold both | 12.5% | 0 → **−140** | 0 → **−49.5** |

Buying separately gives a soft landing in a split and a much worse tail if Republicans hold both. Choose that shape deliberately, not under the name of diversification — sizing it is the subject of [how to size a position](/learn/how-to-size-a-position).

### The trade nothing else expresses

**Republican Senate, Democratic House — 35.5 cents, about a 182% return.**

This is "the swing is real, but it runs out before the fourth seat." Given four narrow leads, a Texas race inside the margin of error, a late nominee in Maine and no spare seat behind them, it is a coherent reading of the same evidence the Democratic side relies on.

**Exit signals:** Talarico opening a clear lead in Texas, Jackson pulling away in Maine, or the generic ballot widening well past D+7. Any of those strengthens the case that the swing clears the map, and this is the first position to come off.

## Why is the arbitrage on this board not real?

There is a combination that looks locked:

1. Buy **Democrats take both** (49.5c)
2. Buy **Republican Senate, Democratic House** (35.5c)
3. Buy **Republican House** (13.5c)

The first two together equal "Democrats win the House", and the third is its complement. Whatever happens, the set pays 100 cents, for **98.5 cents** at midpoints — a 1.5-cent edge.

Now look at the spreads. House YES at 87.5c and House NO at 13.5c sum to **101 cents** to receive 100; the Senate pair, 52.5 and 48.5, also sums to 101. Each binary pair carries about a cent of overround at the midpoint alone, and crossing the spread on three legs costs more than the edge before depth is even counted. A midpoint is not a price you can trade — which is the whole subject of [why orders fill at a worse price](/learn/why-did-my-order-fill-at-a-worse-price).

## Before you trade any of this

1. Read the price on the market page, not from a screenshot.
2. Read the full resolution rules — especially the Vice-Presidency clause in a 50-50 Senate.
3. Look at the ask and the depth, not the midpoint.
4. Size small, and remember that every contract on this board is the same bet on the national swing.
5. Decide your exit signals in advance.
6. There are under eight weeks to 3 November. One poll in Texas or Maine can move this entire board at once — and campaign promises move it less than you would think: [Trump's $5,000 dividend pledge](/learn/trump-5000-dividend-prediction-market) shifted the Republican sweep by about two cents.
